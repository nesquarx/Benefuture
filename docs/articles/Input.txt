[[category:Benefuture]] [[Category:Definitions]]
Input is the process of a [[thing]] absorbing information, energy, or matter from another [[thing]] in [[reality]] surrounding them. Usually resulting in a change in the [[thing]] taking the input.

==Aspects==
* An input, if it does not bring any change to the thing, may not be considered an input.
* A [[being]] often tends to [[interact]] with the world to have control on the inputs it takes.
* Inputs can be processed to extract further types of information.
* Inputs may be taken with or without control of the act of taking or the change it causes.

==Examples==
* Gaining information through any means.
* A raindrop hitting an animal's skin.
* Hearing (absorbing information as pressure waves), seeing (absorbing photons), touching (absorbing information from kinetic displacement of the boundaries of a [[body]]) or any other sensory input.
* Reading (seeing photons and processing their patterns as text).
* To be physically moved by something.
* A change in temperature or pressure in the environment causing a being to react.
* Colliding with another object and taking damage.

==Discussion==
===Information intake and source independence===
Bateson famously defined elementary information as "a difference which makes a difference" to a receiving system.<ref>Bateson, G. (1972). [https://openlibrary.org/works/OL53864W ''Steps to an Ecology of Mind''] (p. 453). Chandler Publishing Company.</ref> In cybernetics and perceptual psychology, an ''input'' is not every physical event in the ambient environment, but specifically the boundary perturbations and signals that couple to an [[entity]]'s internal state.<ref>Ashby, W. R. (1956). [https://openlibrary.org/works/OL1183948W ''An Introduction to Cybernetics''] (Chapter 4). Chapman & Hall.</ref>

When inputs carry epistemic claims through [[communication]], classic social epistemology shows that treating correlated echoes of a single origin as independent witnesses produces cascading overconfidence.<ref>Bikhchandani, S., Hirshleifer, D., & Welch, I. (1992). [https://doi.org/10.1086/261849 A Theory of Fads, Fashion, Custom, and Cultural Change as Informational Cascades]. ''Journal of Political Economy'', 100(5), 992–1026.</ref> Benefuture therefore tags informational inputs with their provenance so repeated readings of the same root source are counted once rather than mistaken for independent confirmation.
