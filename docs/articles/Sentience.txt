Sentience is the quality of an [[entity]] exhibiting [[preference]]s and a threshold of consciousness which perceives or attempts to modify [[reality]] in a self-aware fashion with intent. A sentient entity is called a [[Being]]
==Aspects==
* The definition of consciousness is still evolving<ref>The quest to detect consciousness — in all its possible forms. (2025). ''Nature'', ''643'', 1172–1174. https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-025-02349-5</ref>, but we assume a panpsychic<ref>Goff, Philip, William Seager, and Sean Allen-Hermanson, "Panpsychism", ''The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy'' (Summer 2022 Edition), Edward N. Zalta (ed.), https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/sum2022/entries/panpsychism/</ref> nature of it where it is a sliding scale of different levels ranging from rigid programmed interactions to complete self awareness.
* We assume the threshold of consciousness required to be deemed a sentient being to be at a level where it [[understanding|understands]] itself sufficiently to engage in [[civilization]]. This is soft threshold, there are more detailed [[person#Sentience_thresholds|thresholds]] for personhood.
* Sentient entities can be assumed to hold a level of agency and intent and may be held responsible for their [[interaction]]s.

==Discussion==
===Phenomenal valence and precautionary protection===
In the philosophy of mind, Nagel framed phenomenal consciousness around whether "there is something that it is like to be" an organism—a subjective point of view from which experiences are felt.<ref>Nagel, T. (1974). [https://doi.org/10.2307/2183914 What Is It Like to Be a Bat?]. ''The Philosophical Review'', 83(4), 435–450.</ref> In moral philosophy from Bentham onward, it is specifically ''valenced'' sentience—the capacity to experience states as positive or negative, preferred or avoided ("Can they suffer?")—that grounds protection against [[harm]].<ref>Bentham, J. (1789). [https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/56463 ''An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation''] (Chapter XVII, §1, note). T. Payne and Son.</ref>

Because subjective experience is inferred across diverse biological and synthetic architectures from behavioral, physiological, and structural indicators, Birch argues for a ''precautionary framework'' in animal and artificial sentience law: when credible markers indicate a non-trivial probability of valenced experience, protective thresholds should apply rather than awaiting apodictic certainty.<ref>Birch, J. (2017). [https://doi.org/10.1017/S0953820817000121 Animal Sentience and the Precautionary Principle]. ''Animal Sentience'', 2(16), 1.</ref> Benefuture operationalizes this through graded [[Person|personhood]] thresholds on sentience and by measuring [[happiness]] through integrated behavioral, physiological, and preference-satisfaction channels.
