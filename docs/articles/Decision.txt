[[category:Benefuture]]
A decision or choice is an [[output]] made by an [[entity]] intending to achieve a certain outcome. 
Generally it is a process of:
# [[Understanding]] certain aspects of [[reality]] via [[input]]s.
# Speculating possible outputs and their effects on reality.
# Executing the output which is speculated to affect reality in line with the [[goals]] of the entity.

==Aspects==
* A choice is not made until its declared or acted upon.
* Decisions can change in the midst of execution, but unless the change is a reaction to a change of inputs, it can denote an error in previous speculation.

==Examples==
* A [[person]] takes their own capabilities and the current state of the market as inputs, then creates goals with how they envision the future to be and their interests, speculates on the future needs of the market, then decides to build their career in a way which meet both the needs of the markets and their interests and leads to realizing their goals.
==Discussion==
===Bounded rationality and inaction as a choice===
Classical decision theory models choice as selecting from an available set of actions the option that maximizes expected value under the agent's beliefs.<ref>von Neumann, J., & Morgenstern, O. (1944). [https://openlibrary.org/works/OL3963222W ''Theory of Games and Economic Behavior'']. Princeton University Press.</ref> Because real agents face finite time, attention, and computational capacity, Simon introduced ''bounded rationality'': deliberation is itself a costly resource, so an agent must decide when further search costs more than the expected improvement in the chosen action.<ref>Simon, H. A. (1955). [https://doi.org/10.2307/1884852 A Behavioral Model of Rational Choice]. ''The Quarterly Journal of Economics'', 69(1), 99–118.</ref>

Benefuture incorporates both insights: every decision includes ''no output'' (inaction) inside the option set—so omission and commission are evaluated by the same counterfactual standard rather than privileged by status-quo bias<ref>Samuelson, W., & Zeckhauser, R. (1988). [https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00055564 Status Quo Bias in Decision Making]. ''Journal of Risk and Uncertainty'', 1(1), 7–59.</ref>—and any option whose expected [[harm]] clears the [[freedom]] boundary is filtered before net expected value is maximized.
