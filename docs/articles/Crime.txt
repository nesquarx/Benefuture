A crime occurs when a set of events which can be traced to be triggered by informed volition, or informed inaction against a [[law]]'s mandate or a [[contract]], of one [[entity]] or more (criminal/s) - causes [[harm]] to one or more [[being]]s (victim/s).

==Aspects==
* The crime is of different levels depending upon the degree of harm to the victim, sometimes the degree of possible harm is also taken into consideration.
* The crime is also of different levels depending upon the level of [[understanding]] of the criminal of their actions - specifically their understanding of the possibility of harm to victims while triggering the actions.
* After a crime is discovered, proven and understood, [[justice]] in the form of [[Justice#Reparation|reparation]]s and [[Justice#Rehabilitation|rehabilitation]] for both criminals and victims is necessary.
* Often [[person]]s can be held to a higher standard as criminals than [[being]]s and [[entity|entities]]. Entities, however autonomous, can transfer their criminality to the [[person]]s responsible for the existence of the entity.
* Completed [[Education|infohazard education]] is evidence of being informed for harms within its scope.
* When harm is caused without informed volition or informed inaction against a mandate or contract by any entity, it is called an accident.
* Being informed means demonstrated knowledge, or a recorded exposure to the information; vague standards such as "should have known" are not used, since they are exploitable. Ignorance does not pay because an accident mandates the education that would have informed its contributor, and once exposure is recorded, a repeat is informed.
* An accident's cost to its contributor is bounded by their capacity, the [[administration]] carries the rest, and the contributor takes the [[education]] that would have made them informed.
* A hazard one creates is direct harm: intent decides whether it is accident or crime, and repair happens either way.

==Discussion==
===Mens rea, reckless endangerment, and restorative repair===
Anglo-American and continental criminal jurisprudence have long rested on the maxim ''actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea''—an act does not make a person guilty of a crime unless the mind is also culpable—graduating culpability from purpose and knowledge to recklessness and negligence.<ref>Hart, H. L. A. (1968). [https://openlibrary.org/works/OL1876645W ''Punishment and Responsibility: Essays in the Philosophy of Law''] (Chapter II, "Legal Responsibility and Excuses"). Clarendon Press.</ref> However, because subjective ''mens rea'' is difficult to observe directly, legal theory increasingly grounds culpability in whether the actor had fair notice and a recorded opportunity to avoid the risk.<ref>Alexander, L., & Ferzan, K. K. (2009). [https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511804595 ''Crime and Culpability: A Theory of Criminal Law''] (Chapter 2). Cambridge University Press.</ref>

Benefuture replaces speculative inquiry into hidden mental states with an auditable epistemic test: a harmful action or breach of [[duty]] is a ''crime'' if and only if the actor acted voluntarily while informed (by demonstrated [[understanding]] or a recorded prior warning), and an ''accident'' otherwise. In both cases, victim restitution and restorative repair come first; the distinction governs whether the response is mandatory [[education]] to close an [[ignorance]] gap (for accidents) or [[Justice|rehabilitative restraint]] and escrow ratcheting (for crimes).
