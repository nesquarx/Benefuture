[[category:Benefuture]] [[Category:Definitions]]
A statement is said to be true if it can be [[proof|proven]] to be valid in [[reality]]. If not, the statement is speculation, opinion, or [[untruth]].

==Aspects==
* Truth is not a binary, it is also contextually multi-dimensional, the levels can vary from absolute [[untruth]] to practically absolute truth.
* The same statement can have differing levels of truth in different contexts.
* The level varies with the source and quality of proof.
* Absolute objective truth is unattainable, but worthwhile to pursue.
* The level of truth and its context is an important metadata about any statement.
* Source of information can help estimate the level of truth, but not decide it.
* Unprovable statements cannot be considered as truth of any kind.
==Discussion==
===Correspondence, pragmatism, and weakest-link chains===
Philosophy of truth has long contrasted ''correspondence'' theories—which define truth as structural fidelity between a statement and reality<ref>Tarski, A. (1944). [https://doi.org/10.2307/2102968 The Semantic Conception of Truth: and the Foundations of Semantics]. ''Philosophy and Phenomenological Research'', 4(3), 341–376.</ref>—with ''pragmatic'' theories, which judge a belief by the verifiable predictions and successful actions it enables.<ref>Peirce, C. S. (1878). [https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Popular_Science_Monthly/Volume_12/January_1878/Illustrations_of_the_Logic_of_Science_II How to Make Our Ideas Clear]. ''Popular Science Monthly'', 12, 286–302.</ref> Benefuture unifies the two: because [[reality]] is known through [[interaction]], a model's level of truth is measured by how accurately and invariantly its causal structure predicts outcomes across untested interventions, strictly dominating a black-box curve fit of equal historical accuracy.<ref>Pearl, J. (2009). [https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511803161 ''Causality: Models, Reasoning, and Inference''] (2nd ed., Chapter 1). Cambridge University Press.</ref>

When a conclusion depends on a chain of inferences or premises, Theophrastus's classical ''pejorem'' rule in modal logic holds that a conclusion is no stronger than its weakest premise (''conclusio sequitur partem debiliorem'').<ref>Kneale, W., & Kneale, M. (1962). [https://openlibrary.org/works/OL1370838W ''The Development of Logic''] (Chapter II, §5, p. 101). Clarendon Press.</ref> Benefuture enforces this weakest-link bound on epistemic propagation (`chain_truth_level`): any statement derived from a chain of claims inherits at most the minimum provable truth level of its links, preventing speculative or falsified premises from laundering themselves into high-confidence conclusions.

===In simulation===
* Propagating the weakest-link truth bound across multi-hop citation chains prevents downstream agents from adopting conclusions built on an upstream fabrication, reducing false-claim adoption from 100% to 0% and cutting population belief error by more than 96% ([https://github.com/nesquarx/Benefuture/blob/main/docs/CLAIMS.md the claims record]).
